# Research notes: fixing snoring at home

These notes are the evidence behind the two videos. Each section says what was found, which numbers the videos use, and how much weight the evidence can bear.

**How this was researched.** Searches covered PubMed-indexed trials, systematic reviews, the Cochrane Library and journal pages. The full texts weren't reachable from the build environment, so figures come from abstracts, the publishers' own summaries and indexing records. Where sources disagreed, the video uses the abstract's figures and the disagreement is noted below. Effect sizes are rounded in the narration.

## 1. Mouth and throat exercises (myofunctional therapy): the best home evidence

| Study | Design | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| **Ieto et al., *Chest* 2015;148(3):683–691** | RCT, 39 adults with primary snoring or mild–moderate OSA, 3 months. Control: nasal dilator strips plus breathing exercises | Objectively measured snoring fell in the exercise group: snore index (snores > 36 dB per hour) **99.5 → 48.2** (p = .017), total snore index (total snoring power per hour) **60.4 → 31.0** (p = .033). No significant change in the control group. Bed partners rated snoring intensity and frequency lower. |
| **Camacho et al., *Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol* 2018;275:849–855** | Systematic review and meta-analysis, 9 studies, 211 adults | Snoring decreased on questionnaires and sleep studies. Snoring intensity on a visual analogue scale fell **51%** (8.2 → 4.0, 80 patients). |
| **Abu Alheija et al., *Acta Otolaryngol* 2025;145(9):857–865** | RCT, 125 snorers, 6 weeks, control did finger-coordination exercises | Bed-partner snoring score **2.3 points lower** than control (95% CI 0.6–3.9, p = .007). |
| **Camacho et al., *Sleep* 2015;38(5):669–675** | Meta-analysis in OSA | AHI fell by about half in adults (24.5 → 12.3/h). Snoring time on sleep studies fell (14% → 4% of sleep time). |
| **Guimarães et al., *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2009;179:962–966** | RCT, 31 adults with moderate OSA, ~30 min/day for 3 months | AHI 22.4 → 13.7/h. Snoring frequency and intensity, sleepiness and neck circumference decreased. |
| **Rueda et al., *Cochrane* 2020, CD013449** | Review of 9 RCTs in OSA (347 people) | Myofunctional therapy probably reduces daytime sleepiness compared with sham (Epworth −4.5 points). Most evidence low certainty. |
| **Sperger et al., *Sleep Sci* 2022;15(4):421–428** | RCT, 40 adults with obesity | **No significant change** in objective or subjective snoring. Shown in the video as "not every trial found a benefit". |

**Note on the 2015 trial's numbers.** The American College of Chest Physicians' press release quoted a 36% fall in snoring frequency and a 59% fall in total snoring power. Those don't match the abstract's own index values (about a 52% and 49% fall). The video uses the abstract's values and says "roughly halved".

### The routine in video 2

The core routine is the one from the snoring trial (Ieto 2015): about 8 minutes, three times a day, for 3 months. It's a shortened version of the Guimarães 2009 protocol.

| # | Exercise | Dose in the trial | How the video paces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tongue tip against the front of the palate, slide it backward | 20× | 3.0 s per repetition, counted |
| 2 | Suck the tongue up against the palate, pressing the whole tongue against it | 20× | 3.0 s per repetition |
| 3 | Push the back of the tongue down against the floor of the mouth, tip touching the lower front teeth | 20× | 3.0 s per repetition |
| 4 | Lift the soft palate and uvula while saying "A" intermittently | 20× | 2.5 s per repetition |
| 5 | Finger inside the cheek pushes it outward, cheek muscle (buccinator) squeezes back in | 10× each side | 3.0 s per repetition, 5 s to switch sides |
| 6 | Chewing on alternate sides; swallowing with tongue on the palate, teeth together, lips relaxed | At meals | Shown as a mealtime habit |

Two optional extras come from the longer Guimarães 2009 protocol:

- **Lip press**: lips pressed together with the mouth closed, held for **30 s**. The video shows a 30-second countdown.
- **Balloon**: prolonged breath in through the nose, then a forceful blow into a balloon, **5 times** without taking the balloon out of the mouth. The video paces each breath at 8 s.

The trials didn't publish exact tempos, so the per-repetition timing is the video's own choice: slow enough to move "slowly and firmly". With intros and rests, the core routine takes about 8 minutes, which matches the trial.

## 2. Sleep position

- **Nakano et al., *Sleep* 2003;26(2):169–172.** Non-apneic snorers snored less often and less loudly when lying on their side. In people with OSA, the effect of position on snoring varied.
- **Souza et al., *Sleep Breath* 2017;21:815–820.** In 52 people with OSA, raising the head of the bed to about 7.5° (a 15 cm block under the head-end legs) lowered the AHI from 15.7 to 10.7/h, about a third. Snoring wasn't reported, and the study had no control group, so the video says it "may also help".
- A 2024 systematic review (J Pers Med 14:715) concluded that controlled trials of position change for snoring are still needed.

## 3. Alcohol and sedatives

- **Issa & Sullivan, *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry* 1982;45:353–359.** Small sleep-lab study: alcohol before bed made two habitual snorers develop obstructive apneas in the first hour of sleep.
- **Simou et al., *Sleep Med* 2018;42:38–46.** Meta-analysis of 21 studies: higher alcohol intake was associated with a **25% higher risk** of sleep apnea (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.13–1.38). These are observational studies with high heterogeneity, so they show an association rather than cause.

## 4. Weight and exercise

- **Braver et al., *Chest* 1995;107:1283–1288.** Heavy snorers (men): those who lost ≥ 3 kg went from 320 to 176 snores per hour (**−45%**, p = .0496). Small study.
- **Peppard et al., *JAMA* 2000;284:3015–3021.** 690 adults followed for 4 years: a 10% weight loss predicted a **26% fall** in AHI, and a 10% gain a 32% rise.
- **Kline et al., *Sleep* 2011;34(12):1631–1640.** RCT, 43 adults with OSA. 12 weeks of aerobic plus resistance training lowered the AHI from 32.2 to 24.6/h, against a rise in the stretching control group, with no significant weight loss.

## 5. Nose and smoking

- **Franklin et al., *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2004;170:799–803.** 15,555 adults in five countries: habitual snoring in **24.0%** of current smokers, 20.3% of ex-smokers and **13.7%** of never-smokers. Passive smoking at home was also linked to snoring.
- **Kiely et al., *Thorax* 2004;59:50–55.** In snorers with rhinitis, a nasal steroid spray lowered the AHI and nasal resistance but barely changed snoring loudness. So the video says treating allergies "can make breathing easier", not that it stops snoring.
- **Camacho et al., *Pulm Med* 2016:4841310.** Meta-analysis: nasal dilators improve nasal breathing but didn't significantly change snoring index or AHI in OSA. In the 2015 exercise trial, the nasal-strip control group didn't improve either.

## 6. Wind instruments and singing (small trials)

- **Puhan et al., *BMJ* 2006;332:266–270.** RCT, 25 people with moderate OSA, 4 months of didgeridoo practice (on average 25 min/day, 5.9 days/week). Daytime sleepiness improved (Epworth −3.0) and partners rated sleep disturbance lower (−2.8). The AHI result was borderline (−6.2, p = .05).
- **Hilton et al., *Int J Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2013;2:97–102.** RCT of daily singing exercises for 3 months in snorers and people with mild–moderate OSA: snoring frequency fell. About two thirds kept up the programme.

## 7. Not recommended: mouth taping

- **Rhee et al., *PLOS One* 2025;20(5):e0323643.** Systematic review of 10 studies (213 people). Only two showed a statistically significant improvement, both in mild OSA. Several studies warned of a risk of asphyxiation, especially with a blocked nose. The video recommends skipping it.

## 8. When snoring needs a doctor

Loud snoring with any of the following can mean obstructive sleep apnea:

- someone sees you stop breathing
- choking or gasping in sleep
- strong daytime sleepiness
- high blood pressure

Children who snore most nights should also be checked. These are the standard screening signs (for example the STOP-Bang items). The exercises aren't a substitute for diagnosis or for CPAP when it's needed: the Cochrane review found CPAP lowers AHI more than exercises do.
